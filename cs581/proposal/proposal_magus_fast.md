**Problem.** MAGUS (Smirnov & Warnow, *Bioinformatics* 2021) is one of the most accurate methods for aligning large
datasets. It splits the sequences into subsets, aligns each subset with MAFFT L-INS-i, and merges the subset
alignments with the Graph Clustering Merger (GCM). GCM learns how the subsets line up from ten *backbone* alignments
(200 sequences each, also aligned with L-INS-i), builds an alignment graph from them, clusters it with MCL, and
resolves the clusters into one alignment.

MAGUS is accurate, but it is not fast on modest hardware:
- On a 4-core machine it is only about 12% faster than PASTA on the MAGUS paper's 1,000-sequence DNA data; the
  paper's 2.5× speed-up used 16 cores.
- On BAliBASE proteins it is 2.5–3.3× *slower* than PASTA (14.9 vs 5.9 min and 41.6 vs 12.6 min).

The instructor's project list asks to "improve MAGUS if possible." We ask: **where does MAGUS spend its time, and can
it be made at least 2× faster on DNA, RNA and proteins without losing alignment or tree accuracy?**

**Preliminary observations (from our own timing runs; to be confirmed).**
- *The backbones dominate.* Re-aligning the ten backbones with L-INS-i (timed separately, 4 threads) takes the
  equivalent of roughly 70–95% of MAGUS's total wall time on BAliBASE, ROSE 1000M2 and RNASim 1000. For example,
  BAliBASE BBA0101's backbones take 920–1,040 s, against a 1,090–1,110 s MAGUS run.
- *Graph construction is pure Python* and can be slow on long alignments. We have a vectorized re-implementation
  that builds the identical graph; its end-to-end saving is still to be measured.
- *Pruning weak evidence speeds up the merge step.* Removing graph edges supported by fewer than 4 of the 10
  backbones makes GCM's clustering-and-trace step 2–5× faster (a small part of total time). It also improves protein
  alignment accuracy without hurting DNA.
- *Cheaper backbone aligners are a trade-off, not a free win.* Clustal Omega backbones make MAGUS 3–4× faster on
  BAliBASE but are much less accurate on DNA. MAFFT's fast modes are fast but erratic.

**Research questions.**
1. *Profile.* How is MAGUS's time split (subset alignment, backbones, graph build, MCL, trace) across data types,
   dataset sizes (1,000 to 10,000 sequences) and core counts?
2. *Fewer or smaller backbones.* How many backbones, and of what size, does GCM really need? Does pruning weak
   evidence let MAGUS use fewer or smaller backbones at the same accuracy?
3. *Exact speed-ups.* How much do engineering changes that do not alter the output save end to end: a vectorized
   graph build and parallel MCL?
4. *Combined.* What is the best accuracy–time trade-off curve, and can one setting reach ≥ 2× speed-up at equal
   accuracy on DNA, RNA and proteins? Do the trees from the faster alignments stay as accurate as MAGUS's?

**Approach and evaluation.** We have a harness that runs MAGUS from its own code and reproduces the paper's published
error rates (median difference 0.02 points), plus merge-only reruns that change one component at a time on
identical subsets and backbones.

Data:
- the MAGUS paper's ROSE 1000-sequence conditions, RNASim 1000 and 10K, and 16S;
- BAliBASE RV100 and HomFam proteins;
- simulated proteins (AliSim) with true trees.

Metrics: wall-clock and CPU time end to end (4 and 16 cores where available), peak memory, SP error (SPFN, SPFP;
FastSP), and tree error (FastTree/IQ-TREE nRF vs the true tree) on simulated data. All comparisons are paired, with
several MAGUS runs per dataset (MAGUS varies by about 0.5 points between runs), and W/T/L and Wilcoxon tests.
Baselines: MAGUS (default), PASTA, MAFFT L-INS-i where feasible. For reproducibility, every dataset will be cited by
its public source (Illinois Data Bank DOIs), and every table regenerated by one script.

**Timeline.**
- Week 1: profiling (RQ1); exact speed-ups (RQ3) implemented and verified to give identical output.
- Week 2: backbone number and size sweep with and without evidence pruning (RQ2).
- Week 3: combined settings, end-to-end runs including 10K-sequence data, tree accuracy (RQ4).
- Week 4: write-up.

**Risks.**
- (a) Accuracy may drop with fewer or smaller backbones, especially on hard protein data. The trade-off curve is
  still a useful result even if no setting reaches 2× at equal accuracy.
- (b) Some speed-ups are engineering rather than new algorithms. We will separate "same output, faster code" from
  "different method, faster" and report both.
- (c) Recursive-MAGUS already targets very large inputs. Our focus is the standard MAGUS pipeline at 1,000–10,000
  sequences, where the backbone cost dominates.

**Course connection.** MAGUS, GCM, MCL, divide-and-conquer alignment and SP-error evaluation are covered in the MSA
lectures. The instructor's project list has a "Projects related to MAGUS" section ("The goal here is to improve MAGUS
if possible"). This project keeps MAFFT as MAGUS's aligner and measures every change against MAGUS itself.
